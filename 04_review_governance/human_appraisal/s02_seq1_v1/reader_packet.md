# Candidate A

Four nights after the calibration game, the alignment session ran twenty-three minutes past its promised end. Adrian and Miriam had gone. Mara, Livia, and Jonah remained at the cedar table, the final sequence open across three monitors and the lab door still closed.

Jonah typed with both hands, following the live traces. Livia sat nearest the shared interface, one bare foot hooked behind the leg of her chair. Mara had not eaten since noon, though she had blamed the tremor in her hands on data density. Her bag waited beside her. No one had begun to pack. The raw telemetry remained behind Fulcrum’s permission wall; all Mara could see were summaries.

She watched the clock change by another minute and said nothing.

Livia poured amber tea from a ceramic carafe and slid it across the table.

Mara accepted the warm cup before she could manufacture a reason not to. “Thank you.”

Livia’s eyes narrowed. “Don’t thank me.”

“For the tea.”

“For my attention. That would be presumptuous.”

Mara sipped. “We’re using my attention. What’s the exchange rate?”

Livia smiled as if Mara had named a price. “You’ve been waiting.”

The sensors had been packed away with the puck, but Mara could feel her pulse in the hand around the cup. “I’m new.”

“Not to calibration. You’re waiting for me to make an offer.”

Mara set the cup down firmly enough to prove she had heard. “Is that against the rules?”

“I decide which rules apply.”

“That explains a great deal.”

“You think I’m a tyrant.”

“No. An aristocrat.” Mara glanced at Jonah. “He thinks I’m a tyrant.”

Jonah paused over the keys. “I was trying not to think.”

“Good try.”

He looked toward Livia and found her still watching Mara. He could have carried the joke farther and made the room easier. He returned his attention to the traces instead.

Livia leaned back, hands folded behind her head. “You know what you want.”

The dryness in Mara’s throat had nothing to do with data density. “I’m new.”

Livia nodded, not triumphant, simply certain.

“You think they let you through the gate because you’re clever,” she said. “You’re waiting for the test where you fail to be exceptional. You don’t know that you’re more than capable.”

Mara opened her mouth, closed it, and found no better place to rest it. She kept smiling.

“There’s your proof,” Livia said. “If I were wrong, you’d be saying something.”

Mara picked up the tea and let its heat enter her hands.

“You don’t want to fail,” Livia said. “You want to be found out.”

“I’m new,” Mara repeated.

Livia laughed, softly this time.

Mara drank again. Holding the cup felt like holding a small portion of the room—warm, offered, and not therefore free.

“Fulcrum doesn’t give this to new fellows,” Livia said.

She rotated the shared interface and opened the permissions pane. A new entry appeared line by line: RAW TELEMETRY STREAM. EXPIRES 06:00. ASSIGNEE: MARA VALE. STATUS: PENDING.

Jonah stopped typing. He left both hands visible above the keys.

Livia looked at Mara. “You won’t need a month of training. You can have the raw stream overnight.”

The cup seemed lighter. “It’s not—”

“You could say no. You don’t.”

Mara had hoped Livia would do this. She had been working to prove herself capable when Livia offered her the proof, access no new fellow received and only for as long as Livia’s permission lasted. The night belonged to Fulcrum. The morning, if Mara was good enough to reach it, belonged to Mara.

She looked at the permissions window: the raw stream, her name, Livia’s open expression. “It’s not a reward. It’s the test.”

Livia’s mouth curved. There was real pleasure in being understood and no apology for the terms.

Jonah’s hands remained still. His restraint sharpened every fact Mara might have preferred to blur: the waiting sequence, the closed door, the bag beside her chair. He neither rescued her from the choice nor pretended he had no part in arranging it.

“Next sequence is ready,” he said. Then his fingers returned to position.

The ACCEPT control waited beneath Mara’s name.

She could refuse. She could stand, collect her bag, ask to see the results in the morning. But refusal would enter the room as information: mistrust, insufficient commitment, perhaps failure. Livia already knew that. Jonah knew it now. Neither of them spoke.

Mara asked God for enough freedom to know what she was choosing. Nothing loosened. Instead the cup warmed her palms, the clock changed, and Jonah’s waiting hands became painfully exact.

She tapped ACCEPT before she could make another reason.

Livia turned back to the primary monitor without celebrating. Jonah returned to the traces. The room resumed its ordinary motion around Mara, and she folded her hands in her lap to keep from reaching for anything else.

On the nearest monitor, the next sequence unfurled. Jonah moved one window aside so she could see its timestamps, a practical courtesy that also made her responsible for noticing them. He did not look at her. His restraint might have felt generous if it had not bound him to the same table.

The door stayed shut. At her feet, one strap of her bag had fallen against her ankle. The permission would expire at six.

Her tea cooled.
