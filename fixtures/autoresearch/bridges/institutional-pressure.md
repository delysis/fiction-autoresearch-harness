The tea was exactly the temperature Mara would have chosen for herself, which made refusing it feel theatrical.

Livia set the cup beside Mara's notebook without touching either of them. Chamomile, a slice of ginger, no honey. The accuracy was almost more exhausting than the session.

"You don't have to drink it," Livia said.

Across the conference room, Jonah was collecting the colored cards from the alignment exercise. He did it slowly enough to look accidental. Mara knew an offered witness when she saw one, and resented that she was grateful.

"Then why make it?" she asked.

Livia drew out the chair opposite her. "Because you have been here for four hours, and in the last forty minutes you stopped noticing that you were cold."

Mara became aware of the air-conditioning against the damp back of her blouse. The observation was correct. Its use remained undecided.

She wrapped both hands around the cup but did not lift it. "What happens if I say no to the follow-up?"

"You go to bed. We speak tomorrow."

"And the fellowship review?"

Livia's expression did not change. That was answer enough.

The review form waited between them, already open to the page marked PARTICIPANT REFLECTION. It asked whether Mara had experienced resistance during calibration and whether that resistance felt protective, habitual, or externally induced. Three boxes, each of them turning refusal into another kind of data.

"I don't know what I think yet," Mara said.

"That's why a brief account tonight would help. Memory consolidates around the story we first tell about an event."

"Whose story?"

Livia smiled, tired and genuine. "Ideally yours."

There was the trick: not a lie, not even pressure in the vulgar sense. Livia was offering her the best available chair, the right tea, a scientifically respectable reason to speak now, and the quiet implication that a person serious about truth would be brave enough to do so.

Jonah placed the last card in its box. He did not look at Mara.

She opened her notebook to a blank page.

Livia relaxed by perhaps a millimeter.

Mara wrote the time at the top. Beneath it she drew two columns. In the left she wrote OBSERVATION. In the right she wrote INTERPRETATION.

"What are you doing?" Livia asked.

"Giving you the account you requested."

Under OBSERVATION Mara wrote: Livia brought tea prepared to my preferences. Under INTERPRETATION she wrote: Care. Beneath that: Leverage. She left both words uncircled.

Livia leaned back. For the first time that evening, her attention seemed to arrive half a beat after Mara's action rather than before it.

"That's fair," she said.

"I haven't evaluated fairness."

"No." Livia looked at the form, then closed it. "You haven't."

The small concession brought no surge of victory. Mara was too tired for victory, and Livia was not a machine that stopped exerting force when correctly named. She was a woman who had noticed Mara shivering and wanted an answer before morning for reasons that were probably mixed even inside herself.

"I can give you ten minutes of observations," Mara said. "No interpretation tonight. No diagnostic labels. You don't quote me in the review meeting without showing me the quotation first."

"The review is at eight."

"Then you can show me at seven-thirty."

Livia glanced toward Jonah. He kept his eyes on the card box, refusing the invitation to certify either woman.

"All right," Livia said.

"Say the terms."

A trace of irritation crossed her face. It made Mara trust the agreement more.

"Ten minutes. Observations only. No quotation without your approval."

Mara set the timer on her phone and turned it so both of them could see.

She began with the red card. The participant had guessed fear after Mara changed her breathing, then guessed calm when she changed only the angle of her chin. Livia asked one question about timing. Mara answered with a timestamp rather than an explanation. Each time Livia reached toward meaning, Mara tapped the right-hand column with her pen.

At six minutes, Livia stopped reaching.

At nine, Mara described the one result she could not explain: the guess that had remained accurate when she had changed every cue she knew how to change. Saying it aloud made the room feel briefly larger, not smaller. Mystery survived the loss of Livia's certainty.

The timer sounded.

Livia looked at the unfinished page. Mara watched care and appetite contest behind her composure.

"Good night," Livia said at last.

Mara drank the tea. It was still warm.

Jonah held the door while she gathered her notebook, but did not turn the courtesy into an alliance. As Mara passed him, she saw that he had left one colored card on the table: white, the card for insufficient evidence.

She did not know whether the omission was deliberate.

For once, not knowing felt less like failure than room.
