The rain had stopped, but the magnolia leaves still released it one drop at a time.

Mara stood under the old carriage arch with Jonah and listened to the compound empty itself behind them. A door shut in the lab. Another opened in the fellows’ house. Fulcrum was good at making privacy feel temporary.

“You don’t have to walk me back,” she said.

“Correct.”

He stayed where he was.

She looked at him. “That answer usually precedes a person doing the unnecessary thing anyway.”

“I’m trying a new protocol. The subject specifies what would help. The technician refrains from converting her answer into hidden data.”

“Sounds underfunded.”

“No military applications.”

His hands were visible, one holding his jacket, the other empty at his side. She knew he had arranged that. The knowledge warmed her and irritated her in equal measure: he had noticed what the closed room had done to her, and he had managed to notice without announcing her to herself.

Mara stepped out from beneath the arch. Gravel shifted under her shoe. Her legs had the watery unreliability that followed too much concentration and too little food.

Jonah moved half a pace, then stopped before the motion became assistance.

“There,” she said. “That.”

“Bad?”

“No.” She disliked how quickly the answer came. “Ask me.”

He offered his free hand, palm up. “Would you like something steady?”

The hand was broad and ordinary. A faint dark line of machine grease remained beside his thumbnail. Nothing about it claimed to know her.

Mara put her hand in his.

His fingers closed only after hers did. The contact sent a clean awareness up the inside of her arm—not the manufactured electricity of the calibration game, with twenty people waiting to interpret her pulse, but the startling fact of being held by someone who expected her to remain capable of leaving.

They walked.

The path narrowed beside the kitchen garden. Wet rosemary gave off its sharp green smell when Mara’s skirt brushed it. Jonah’s thumb rested against the side of her hand and did not move. The restraint became, after several steps, more distracting than movement would have been.

“You can use your thumb,” she said.

“For balance?”

“For whatever dissertation you’re writing with it.”

His laugh was quiet. His thumb crossed her knuckle once.

Mara missed a step she had already seen.

Jonah steadied her without pulling her closer. “Experimental result.”

“Confounded by gravel.”

“Naturally.”

At the fellows’ house, the porch light had burned out again. The darkness made his face less readable and his attention more so. Mara could feel it in the pause between them, in the way his hand loosened without quite letting go.

She thought of Livia’s certainty. You want him to make goodness simple for you.

Jonah had not made anything simple. He had refused to tell her whether the session was harmless, whether Livia was dangerous, whether Mara was brave. He had walked beside her and let every unanswered question retain its teeth.

“Thank you,” she said.

“For the hand, or for not having a useful opinion?”

“The second was almost impressive.”

“I can ruin it if you need closure.”

“I’m learning to tolerate ambiguity.”

“At Fulcrum? Radical.”

She smiled. He watched her smile, and for once neither of them pretended that observation was neutral.

Mara lifted their joined hands until his knuckles touched the front of her coat. Jonah’s breath changed. It was small enough that Livia would have made a cathedral of it.

“I noticed that,” Mara said.

“I assumed you might.”

“Any interpretation to offer?”

“Conflict of interest.”

The answer left the choice with her. It also made her want to be less careful than she had been all evening.

She reached for his jacket with her other hand and drew him the remaining inch.

The first touch of his mouth was warm and questioning. Mara answered before the question could become polite. His free hand came to her waist, stopped there, and waited. When she leaned closer it tightened, not enough to keep her, enough to tell her he had understood.

The kiss deepened by agreements too small for language: the angle of his head, her hand opening against his chest, his breath catching when her teeth touched his lower lip. Desire gathered without making either of them stupid. She knew where the porch step was. She knew the lab windows behind them were dark. She knew his restraint was costing him because she could feel the effort in the stillness of the hand at her waist.

Mara broke the kiss.

Jonah did not follow. His forehead rested lightly against hers, his breathing uneven.

“Still want to?” he asked.

“Very much.”

“Good.”

She laughed once, breathlessly. “That is a strange response to stopping.”

“I’m relieved the variable wasn’t lack of interest.”

“The variable is that I would like tomorrow to remain mine.”

His hand eased from her waist. The loss of pressure was immediate and bright.

“Then tomorrow remains yours,” he said.

It was not a vow, and it did not solve Fulcrum. It was better: a choice he could be observed making while he still wanted something else.

Mara touched her thumb to his mouth, once, because stopping did not require pretending the kiss had been a mistake.

“Walk me to breakfast,” she said.

“Is that a date?”

“It’s a replication.”

“Naturally.”

She let go of his hand and went inside with desire intact, the unanswered institution behind her, and one new piece of evidence she was willing to trust until morning.
