Livia poured amber tea from a ceramic carafe and slid it to Mara without asking.

The warm cup fit in Mara’s hand and felt good.

“I’m fine,” she said.

“Fine is not interesting.” Livia picked up a third cup and started to pour for herself. “It’s fine that lets us make assumptions we later have to recover from. But you’re not fine, because I asked.”

Mara looked at the tea, then at Jonah. He continued to read the live traces rather than choose between the two women.

She drank a mouthful and felt the warmth reach her stomach.

Livia added honey to her own tea, her attention on the glass. “What do you think your father would say if he saw you now?”

Mara waited, watching her pour. “That I’ve eaten dinner with strangers.”

“That you’re tired and hungry and letting an authority figure choose the amount of rest you receive. That your choice is between doing a good job here and returning to your congregation as the woman who couldn’t do the work. That you are so worried about being found ordinary that you’re letting yourself become exhausted in order to prove that you’re not. That if this is a real problem, you should have brought it up already. That the kindest way to protect your reputation is to stay silent and keep working until your mind falls apart, because if you talk about the stress, it will mean you’re weak.”

Mara tried not to drink another mouthful.

Livia looked at her.

“You could have said that quietly,” Mara said.

“You would have interpreted it as kindness. You wouldn’t have believed it. And I would have believed you didn’t believe me. That would have been a game we played until neither of us was sure where the truth lay.”

“My father doesn’t say things like that.”

“Your father is not the problem.” Livia put down the honey jar. “It’s not about him.”

“It’s about me.”

“We both know that.”
