THEY CAME THROUGH THE BOGGY CREEK-BED, two men and a horse. The man on horseback carried a third body across his pommel. The dead man’s head lolled forward, one eye open and milky under the grime on his face; he had been dead for many hours.
It was June 1755. They had come all the way from the headwaters of the Kanawha River, following the old trail along the crest of the Alleghanies, a long journey by horseback. And they had been on horseback for no more than a week, much of the way on foot. The dead man, an Indian, had been carried most of the distance on Ian Murray’s shoulders.
Now they stopped at a muddy pool in the stream-bed, one of the horses lying down and dipping his muzzle in the water, the other drinking from a spring welling up through the muck. One of the men stood with his back to a willow, arms crossed on his chest and head bent to the earth. The other sat with his back to a boulder, feet braced against the root of a sapling, chin resting on his chest. Both men looked worn, and wore their clothes in rags.
Ian Murray and John Quincy Myers, home from the dead.
THEY HAD COME BACK from the dead before. Not once but twice. Yet this was different from any previous return; this was the final homecoming.
