The first few weeks in the new lab are a blur of chaos, as if everyone has been simultaneously given permission to get lost in a giant, unfamiliar amusement park, then forgotten.

We’re still without a permanent team, because we’re waiting to find out if any of the other lab candidates will make it in the end. There’s some concern about one of them; the woman keeps showing up in different departments, sometimes in different cities and countries, and I hear people mutter in the corridor that maybe she shouldn’t be in the program.

The more I learn, the more I appreciate the way Fulcrum works. The company’s organization is based on an actual science—a science of people, called psychometry—that allows them to keep the same people and teams together for decades. They have what seem like dozens of specialties and departments, but they’re all really just different applications of the same principles. The theory is, if they keep moving you to departments that work better for your strengths, then you stay sharp and you stay excited.

I don’t have the same kind of psychometric analysis that they do on their employees, but they have done the best they can on me, and from what I hear, my results are all over the map.

The team leader, a guy named Robert, is in the room when I get there, so we introduce ourselves. He’s about forty-five, with close-cropped gray hair.
