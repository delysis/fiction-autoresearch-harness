Mara

My friend was right; the Fulcrum isn’t a job. It’s a condition. The more time I spend inside this room with the two chairs, the more I realize that I am not really working for them; instead, they work for me. Or on me.
My mother sent me to them—I couldn’t tell you what exactly for—with instructions to give them the password she gave me. She’s never said more.
They sent me back to my school in a public school bus, with no money to pay my fare, my school uniform still on but my clothes stolen, and a week’s worth of memory wiped clean from my life.
My mother says that she did not realize they would do that. She says I should be thankful for their services, since she has no money to pay them. And if she can’t pay, what other price can I expect them to exact?
And, in any case, it has been working, hasn’t it? They haven’t done it again. I haven’t lost any more memories, or time. It’s been six months. I go to them, once a week. They ask their questions, and I give my answers. And they help me, with no payment from me or my mother, to make sure that I have the only family I need.
So now I walk the twelve blocks to the lab, because I’m not allowed
