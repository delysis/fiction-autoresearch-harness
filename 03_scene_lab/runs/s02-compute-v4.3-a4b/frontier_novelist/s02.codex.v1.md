Four nights later, at 1:17, the west projector went to sleep. Its fan spun down, leaving one fewer sound between Mara and the rain.

Nobody took this as a sign from God.

Livia took it as permission to stop pretending they were nearly finished. She pushed back from the console and went to the sideboard, where an electric kettle had been holding the same water at the same temperature for six hours. The pot she chose was brown ceramic, round-bellied and ugly. Mara liked it at once.

“Give me your cup.”

“I don’t have one.”

“Of course you don’t.” Livia found one beneath a stack of paper napkins. “You have opinions for twenty people and no plan to feed yourself.”

She poured. The tea smelled of toasted rice. Mara accepted it before she could decide whether acceptance was wise, warming both hands against the cup while Livia folded a napkin into a precise square and set two salted almonds on it.

“Those are a ration?” Mara asked.

“Those are the last two. I have loved you sacrificially.”

It drew the laugh Livia had been after. Her answering smile was tired and pleased, with none of the polished radiance she used in the circle.

Then Livia glanced at the untouched almonds.

“You’re afraid you came here with a parlor trick.”

Mara looked into the tea. One rice grain had escaped its bag and was turning slowly in the amber water.

“That’s a bold interpretation for a Tuesday.”

“Wednesday.”

“Worse.”

Livia sat on the edge of the table beside her. She did not lower her voice. The room was too exhausted for secrecy; three people were still working, and Jonah’s terminal threw a blue bar across his hands.

“You watched me miss you this afternoon,” Livia said. “You enjoyed it for about thirty seconds. Then you started wondering whether anybody with decent eyesight could do what you did.”

This was close enough that Mara wanted to correct it. The enjoyment had lasted longer.

She ate one almond to occupy her mouth.

“I wondered whether the method could be taught,” she said.

“That is the respectable version.” Livia nudged the second almond toward her. “The other version is that you crossed the country to discover you were exceptional, and now you’re furious with me for making excellence look learnable.”

Mara felt the answer arrive in her face. Livia saw it. She had the grace not to smile.

Across the room, Jonah’s cursor stopped. His fingers remained on the keys.

Mara set the cup down carefully. “If you’re planning to bill me for this insight, I should warn you that my insurance considers Fulcrum out of network.”

“I’m planning to give you something.”

Livia turned Mara’s tablet around. Mara’s hand stopped halfway back to the cup. A Fulcrum permission window occupied the screen.

TEMPORARY REVIEW ACCESS: RAW ALIGNMENT TELEMETRY

Grantee: MARA VALE

Expires: 06:00

Below it, in smaller type, the system listed pulse traces, room audio, observer annotations, and the unfiltered wrist-contact stream. Material Mara had been told would remain restricted until the research cohort finished its first month.

A gold key shone beside her name. Until tonight she had seen it beside Adrian’s and Livia’s. Beneath the key: AUTHORIZED BY LIVIA SLOANE. SPONSOR ASSUMES REVIEW LIABILITY.

“You said the channel could be tested,” Livia said. “Test it.”

Mara could already see the comparison: Livia’s spoken reads against the time-coded changes in breath, posture, pressure. By morning she might know where accuracy ended.

At the bottom of the window, a line blinked red.

REVIEW NOTES DUE BEFORE EXPIRATION.

Mara looked up. “You’re assigning me an overnight diagnostic.”

“I’m giving you access.”

“With a six o’clock deliverable.”

“Adrian reads the first summary at seven.” Livia’s thumb moved once over the seam of her skirt. “I had to put a name on it.”

The seam had creased under Livia’s thumb.

“The sequence is ready,” Jonah said.

His hands had left the keyboard. He was looking at the permission window, not at Mara. The old irony was gone from his voice. On his monitor, the button that would load the next run remained gray. His right thumb was buried in his left palm, whitening the nail. Mara had the absurd urge to cross the room and pry it loose. He had never looked less like refuge.

Mara wanted him to make it easier. A joke would have done it. An eye roll, a muttered warning, any small theft of gravity. He gave her none.

“Does raw mean raw?” she asked him.

Jonah’s mouth shifted, almost a smile. “It means nobody has explained it yet.”

That was worse. That was irresistible.

Mara touched ACCEPT.

The terminal beside Jonah chimed. A new column opened beneath her name, already counting down from four hours and forty-two minutes. Livia picked up the empty pot and returned it to the sideboard. She did not thank Mara or congratulate her. She only brought back the kettle and added hot water to the cup, though Mara had not drunk half of what was there.

Jonah called up the next sequence. Around them, the surviving members of the cohort pulled their chairs toward the monitors.

Mara folded her hands in her lap. The tea cooled between her wrists while her name glowed on three screens.

The new sequence opened with Mara’s pulse.

A red line crossed Jonah’s monitor at seventy-eight beats per minute, climbed to eighty-six when he moved his chair beside hers, and settled at eighty-three when he stopped. Under the trace, an observer field populated itself.

01:19:08 — SUBJECT ORIENTS TOWARD J. REED UNDER LOAD.

01:19:11 — PROBABLE ATTACHMENT-SEEKING RESPONSE.

Mara read the second line twice.

“Who is writing those?”

Jonah looked at Livia. It was quick, and it was enough.

“The annotation layer is collaborative,” Livia said. “Raw doesn’t mean context-free.”

“Then it isn’t raw.”

“Nothing involving a human observer is raw. That was your point this afternoon.” Livia sounded almost proud of her. “Now you can see the observers too.”

The answer was good. Mara hated that it was good.

Jonah placed a sensor puck between them. The plastic casing had a split beside the charging port, mended with a crooked line of silver epoxy. He turned it so the indicator faced Mara and kept his hands away.

“It needs a baseline,” he said.

“Whose?”

“Yours.”

“That was nearly an answer.”

The corner of his mouth moved. On the monitor, her pulse rose to eighty-nine.

A third annotation appeared.

01:20:02 — HUMOR DEFLECTS AROUSAL/STATUS ANXIETY.

Heat ran from Mara’s collar to her ears. Livia had not needed to invent that. The line was there, in clean white type, while Jonah sat close enough for Mara to see the pale crescent pressed into his palm by his thumbnail.

A hexagon at the end of the entry identified its authorship.

LS / ASSISTED COMPLETION 38%.

“Which thirty-eight percent?” Mara asked.

Livia bent over the monitor. A loose strand of her hair brushed Mara’s shoulder. “The system offered status anxiety. I accepted it.”

“And arousal?”

“Mine.”

She said it without coyness and returned to her chair. Mara’s pulse climbed another two beats, an answer to something, though the annotation field stayed empty.

“Delete it,” Jonah said.

Livia’s chair creaked behind them. “Why?”

“Because it’s two guesses joined by a slash.”

“Then add a third.”

He did not touch the keyboard.

The red line reached ninety-four.

Mara could have explained the rise. She could have told them what the slash concealed, what the warmth of Jonah’s knee near hers was doing, how anger moved through the body with some of the same equipment as desire. The explanations gathered behind her teeth, each one accurate enough to become another entry.

She closed her mouth.

Livia came around the console and crouched beside her. Up close, the long night showed at the corners of her eyes. “You wanted to know whether the readings survived inspection,” she said. “Stay with it. Don’t abandon the test when it reaches material you care about.”

Mara cared about the test. She cared about Livia’s faith in her. She cared, mortifyingly, that Jonah had asked for the line to be deleted before Mara had found the courage to object.

The permission countdown changed to 04:36:12.

“Read the next prompt,” Livia said.

On Mara’s tablet a sentence appeared.

I FEEL SAFER WHEN OTHER PEOPLE EXPLAIN ME.

Below it: AGREE / DISAGREE / FURTHER INQUIRY.

There was no blank space.

Mara pressed two fingers against the warm cup. The prayer she wanted was an argument with better lighting. She let it go.

*Jesus, I am here. You are here. What is mine to do?*

Nothing in her softened. Her face still burned. Jonah was still beside her; Livia was still waiting; the sentence remained clever and false in at least three directions.

Mara waited through one breath anyway. In church, people were always rushing to furnish God’s silences. She had learned to leave one bare.

But the screen had other things on it.

In the lower-right corner, beneath the countdown, was a gray link she had not noticed while planning her analysis.

PARTICIPANT CONTROLS.

Mara opened it.

Livia stood. “Those are administrative.”

“So is my name.”

Three options appeared: PAUSE STREAM, CORRECT IDENTITY, WITHDRAW CONSENT.

The last option carried a warning.

WITHDRAWAL ENDS THE ACTIVE TRIAL AND FLAGS ALL SUBSEQUENT COLLECTION FOR REVIEW.

Mara’s fingertip hovered. The room was completely awake now. Even the two analysts at the far table had stopped pretending not to listen. Miriam, who had been logging the session from the side console, took off her glasses.

“If you withdraw in the middle of a response spike,” Livia said, “you will make the record impossible to interpret.”

“No,” Mara said. “I’ll make it impossible to finish.”

Livia’s hurt arrived before her composure covered it. Mara saw the woman who had put her own name beneath the gold key, who had brought tea and two almonds and an invitation into the locked rooms of the work.

Mara touched WITHDRAW CONSENT.

The pulse trace stopped at ninety-one.

Jonah’s monitor displayed a red authorization request.

OPERATOR ACKNOWLEDGMENT REQUIRED.

Jonah read it but did not move. The request waited beneath his name.

Mara looked at Livia. “Is my no something you’re going to annotate, or is it what happens next?”

Livia’s mouth opened. No answer came.

Behind them, the cedar door remained closed. No one had touched the latch. The red request stayed on Jonah’s screen, and the stopped pulse line stayed flat on Mara’s.

Miriam put her glasses on the table.

She crossed the room without looking at Livia and read Mara’s screen. The witness field had appeared beneath the operator request.

“If you sign that,” Livia said, “you’re no longer neutral.”

“I’m not neutral.” Miriam entered her name.

“You’re assigned to observe this cohort.”

“Then you should assign somebody else.”

Her thumb met the reader. The witness field turned green.

Jonah’s request remained red.

Livia faced him. Her gaze moved from the red request to his hand. “You can leave it for Adrian.”

“That would continue collection until he wakes up.”

“It would preserve the record.”

Jonah clicked ACKNOWLEDGE.

Every monitor went black. A message appeared on his terminal before it followed them.

OPERATOR ACCESS SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW.

He took his hand from the mouse. The crescent in his palm had deepened to red.

Livia did not look at Mara. She picked up the permission tablet, saw the same three names Mara had seen, and held it against her ribs. “Go, then.”

Mara stood. Her knees objected. Miriam was already at the cedar door, one hand on the latch and her observer badge in the other. When she opened it, rain-cooled air entered the room and lifted the hair along Mara’s arms.

Mara stopped beside Livia.

“You were right,” she said. “I did come here to find out whether I was special.”

Livia’s gaze rose to hers.

“You’re welcome,” Livia said.

It was so perfectly Livia that Mara nearly laughed. Instead she touched two fingers to the tablet between them, a small acknowledgment of the risk still attached to Livia’s name, and walked out.

The rain had stopped. Water collected at the tips of the redwood needles and released in heavy drops, striking the pavilion roof long after the weather had moved east. Mara crossed the deck and started down the gravel path toward the residence cottages.

Jonah caught up without calling her name.

For twenty yards, only their shoes spoke. His left one had acquired a squeak. Mara waited to see whether it would become endearing or intolerable.

“You built the annotation layer,” she said.

“Part of it.”

“The part with the slash?”

“The part that records who accepted the suggestion after the slash.”

“Comforting distinction.”

“It was, when I wrote it.”

His phone vibrated. He silenced it without looking.

Mara stopped beneath a redwood whose trunk was wider than her childhood bedroom. “Don’t give me the version that makes you sound restrained. I’ve had enough interpretations for one night. Just tell me what you did.”

Jonah rubbed the heel of his hand over his mouth.

“Three weeks ago I found observer text appearing in exports labeled raw. Sometimes it was cached. Sometimes it was generated after the session and written backward into the session view. I filed it as a security problem.”

“And?”

“I filed anonymously.”

The squeaking shoe, the crooked epoxy on the sensor puck, the careful gray button: all at once he seemed less composed and more assembled.

“So they ignored it.”

“They thanked an unnamed colleague for the interesting edge case.”

“And you let me accept access to it.”

“Yes.”

There was no defense in the word. It made anger easier.

Mara resumed walking. “Why the pressure on my wrist that first day?”

“Your pulse was visible on the monitor.”

“That’s how you timed it. Why did you do it?”

He took three more steps before answering. “You looked like you were disappearing. I wanted to give you one thing in the room that wasn’t asking to mean something.”

Mara turned on him. “It meant something.”

“I know.”

“You keep doing that.”

“Doing what?”

“Standing very still and hoping it counts as innocence.”

He flinched. Not much. Enough.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

His apology did not return his access or Miriam’s assignment. It did not unwrite the annotations.

He opened the anonymous report on his phone and held it out to her. The author field was blank. Mara did not take it yet.


Mara became aware of his shirt where the rain had darkened one shoulder, of the clean soap at his collar, of his bare hand hanging uselessly beside hers. Anger had not displaced attraction. It had given it edges.

She caught the front of his shirt in her fist. The fabric was rough and cool.

His breath stopped.

“I’m going to kiss you,” she said.

“Please.”

She pulled him down.

The first contact was crooked. His nose struck her cheek; Mara laughed into his mouth, and then neither of them was laughing. His hand came to her waist and stayed there, open and warm through her sweater. He kissed as he had held her wrist—steadily at first, as though steadiness could keep desire from becoming visible. It could not.

Mara felt the moment his care lost its polish. His fingers tightened. The sound he made was small and astonished. Heat opened low in her body, clean as hunger, and for several breaths she wanted every next thing at once.

She flattened her palm against his chest.

Jonah stopped.

His hand left her waist. He stepped back far enough that she could have changed her mind without having to move him. His jaw worked once. He did not look disappointed; he looked awake.

Mara kept hold of his shirt.

“I want to know which part of this is you in daylight,” she said.

“That seems unfair to daylight.”

“It has advantages. Breakfast, for example.”

“Are you asking me to breakfast?”

“I’m assigning you an early diagnostic.”

His laugh came out rough. “Six?”

“Seven. I’m not Livia.”

“I had noticed.”

Seven put him in the future: washed, rested, answerable. She wanted him there.

She released his shirt, one finger at a time.

“If either of us says raw,” Mara said, “we stop.”

“Stop what?”

“Whatever is happening. Then the other person asks instead of annotating.”

“Raw,” Jonah said.

Mara looked at him.

“Can I walk you the rest of the way?”

“Yes.”

They had gone three steps when his phone vibrated again. This time he looked.

The color left his face.

“What?”

He handed her the phone.

FULCRUM PHYSICAL SECURITY — UNRESOLVED EVENT

PAVILION WEST DOOR: OPEN

TIME: 01:17:00

CREDENTIAL SOURCE: —

Mara looked back. Through the trees, the pavilion windows burned amber. The west door was the cedar door Miriam had opened at 1:34. At 1:17, the projector had gone dark and everyone had still been inside.

“Could the clock be wrong?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Could somebody have opened it?”

“Yes.”

“Did they?”

Jonah looked at the blank credential field.

“That,” he said, “is raw.”
