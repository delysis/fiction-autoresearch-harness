A rideshare leaves Mara with one suitcase at Fulcrum's redwood-and-glass Northern California compound before an evening welcome. Mara is the only viewpoint. Unusual cue-reading always retains ordinary sensory and behavioral explanations.

Mara's phone is set to play a new audio book once the drive starts. The software, she can tell, does not understand that Fulcrum is a place, even an important place, and it is already dark out. Mara wants to look, but her phone will make noise when she touches it.

The car's navigation voice is male, because most drivers are male, and its software thinks of this stretch as a road to the city rather than a return trip along the coast. The voice counts down to the first turn and then gives an address that Mara has known for weeks. It gives the name of the next turn in less than a mile.

The driver, with both hands on the wheel, looks at the left turn in his rearview mirror before switching on the turn signal.

Mara's phone starts its audio book as soon as it sees the turn.

In the light of the car, the gatehouse's redwoods look old, their trunks wider than Mara has expected. There are two signs outside the fence that say Fulcrum Institute and its address. One sign is higher than the other. The driver is not following directions but driving on automatic memory: he does not see the signs or the lights.

Mara asks for the gate to be left open. The driver presses a button near the glovebox and says "No way" to himself. Mara's phone, in her lap, does not look like a book, but it makes an audiobook noise for several minutes after her car stops.

There is a long minute in which both driver and passenger pretend that neither has seen the other. The passenger opens the rear door from the outside and picks up the suitcase handle while the driver picks up his phone. He asks in a quiet voice how much of this is left. "No way," he says again, as if his answer might have changed.
