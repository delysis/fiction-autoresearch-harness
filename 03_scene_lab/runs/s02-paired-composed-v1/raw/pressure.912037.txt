Mara had stopped listening at seventeen. Now she kept her face relaxed enough that no one who glanced up could identify which side channel had captured her.

On the left, a man named Seth described the difficulties of training a large language model on a dataset whose only available feature was absence.

“It’s like doing math in a room,” Seth said, “where the walls are made of mist and you have to write in water.”

“A theological metaphor,” someone said.

“It’s a metaphor I can understand,” Mara said.

The side comment earned a glance from Livia, who had moved to the window seat near the back. She sat with one leg tucked under her, writing on a tablet, and watched the room through the glass without appearing to.

“I’m going to walk a little,” Mara said to the person beside her.

“You want a coffee?” he asked.

“You want to walk?”

He did. They left together.

The man had a name and a project, but the name and project merged at the door. He pointed out a flaw in the building’s design—three kinds of glass, no unifying aesthetic—and then pointed out another. Mara’s attention had left him behind. She could feel it in her shoulders, the way they eased away from him at each turn, and her stomach, which did not twist when he walked a step ahead and faced her down the path.

By the time he began to describe his model’s alignment problems, Mara was ready to answer that it was probably the water. She held the comment at bay by putting the water bottle to her lips, then discovered that the water was gone.

The man laughed and took out his own bottle. Mara drank his and gave it back without comment.

“Are you okay?” he asked.

“Fine.”

“Because you’re really quiet tonight.”

Mara could hear every word he had said about his model and none of the others. The difference had to mean something.

He stopped walking. She did not notice until he was a step behind her.

“Mara?” he said.

She turned.

“I’m fine.”

“You don’t look it.”

He was looking at her face, but he seemed to believe he could find something else under it.

“I’m tired,” she said. “A little burned out. It happens.”

He stared at her. “I didn’t say you did anything wrong.”

Mara met the accusation. “It didn’t occur to me that you would.”

The man took a breath and stopped there, as though the air might have made a point for him.

Mara waited. She had once told a man who had been trying to convince her that the fellowship’s gender ratios were fair that men often thought the first part of a sentence completed it. She had wondered at the time why it had seemed worth saying. She had forgotten to wonder what had happened to the man after that.

“You know what I think?” he asked.

“You’re about to tell me.”

“I think you’re better than the people you’re trying to be.”

Mara’s hand tightened around the water bottle. “I didn’t think I was trying to be anyone.”

“I see you here. I see how you work, how you think. You’re better than that.”

He stepped closer as he said it. Mara did not move back.

“Maybe I am,” she said. “Maybe I’m better than them.”
