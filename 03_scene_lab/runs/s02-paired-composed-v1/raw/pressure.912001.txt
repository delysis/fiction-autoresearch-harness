Livia’s promised demonstration had run an hour past that.

The small conference room in the pavilion had been arranged for an afternoon seminar. The sensor puck lay on the low table between the chairs, and the live traces remained visible on the screen behind Livia. Mara sat near the door with her phone resting face down on the cedar table. It had been fifteen minutes since the last break and three since Adrian had checked his watch.

Miriam sat at the side console and made no attempt to conceal her boredom. Jonah sat against the far wall and made no attempt to conceal his amusement. Mara did not ask whether she should leave. The session was part of Livia’s effort to turn her down without offering a refusal. Mara wanted to be a fool for as long as the game lasted.

Livia paused beside the puck. She spread her fingers in a small arc, then paused again to lift one leg behind the other. Her pulse did not change, her breathing barely changed, and the machine said her left calf was now holding forty-eight percent of her weight.

“You’re hiding something,” she told the sensor.

Mara could not locate the change. Across the room, Miriam wrote into her log with one hand and typed with the other.

“The room is hot,” Mara said.

Jonah laughed. Livia smiled, then turned back to the screen.

“The room is hot,” she told it. “I am keeping my feet farther apart than the person I am pretending to be would. That person is a woman named Mrs. O’Connell.”

The room did not laugh. Mara had not meant it as a joke.

“Mrs. O’Connell,” she repeated.

“That is a very specific name.”

Livia shrugged. “The room is hot,” she told the screen. “I am keeping my feet farther apart than Mrs. O’Connell would. That person has three daughters, one husband, and one house of her own.”

That was not specific enough. Mara let the thought complete before she looked away.

The session ended at last. Miriam copied the traces to a secure drive and logged out. Jonah disconnected the sensor and gave it to Mara.

She touched it. It was warm but not unpleasantly so.

“It gets warmer the closer she comes to a match,” Jonah said.

Livia remained seated. Her hair had darkened against the nape of her neck, and the pulse trace on her wrist showed no sign of slowing. Mara wished she had brought something besides curiosity.

“Are you hungry?” Livia asked.

“Maybe.”

“Definitely, then.” Livia stood. Her dress moved without a sound. “I’ll put in a delivery order. You can say what you want on the way down.”

Mara could not refuse an order. She could say what she wanted on the way to Livia’s house, and she would be polite. Livia might have left a space for refusal in the plan; she might have intended the invitation from the beginning; or she might have been hungry enough to offer food without noticing that it removed Mara’s last excuse.

The walk took twelve minutes.

Livia’s house had been an old farm before Fulcrum renovated it. In photographs the main room had looked huge. Here it was too large for one person and not quite large enough for a dinner party. The walls held pictures that were both too many and not quite enough.
