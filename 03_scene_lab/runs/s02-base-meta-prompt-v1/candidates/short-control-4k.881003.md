Mara waited for the team to file past the kitchenette and take the outside stairs before she began loading the coffee grounds into the compost. The morning had been full of things she already knew: the two-dimensional assumptions built into a time-series model; the error introduced when you fitted a spatial process to a time series; the error introduced when you fit anything to a time series. She had learned those things in the second semester of her third year.

She had not learned how a man could write a paper about human behavior in an open environment while treating it as a set of data points in a closed one.

She had not learned how the man who did it could sound persuasive.

Livia had led the critique and, with it, the session. Adrian sat at the head of the table in an ordinary sweater, listening to the team dismantle his favorite kind of argument. Jonah sat beside Mara without making her feel that he had done it on purpose.

Livia read the paper from a tablet while the others marked up their own copies. She paced between the table and the far wall, asking questions to which the answers were already visible: the wrong graph, the misleading claim of universality, the hidden assumption of stasis. The team answered with graphs, counterclaims, and evidence of change.

Mara was glad Livia did not ask her.

When the session ended, the team packed up and left in twos and threes. Livia stayed, reviewing a document on her phone. Mara looked over the top of the coffee grounds into the kitchen’s second-hand coffee maker and decided that the water needed fresh ice.

She opened the fridge door. Jonah was there before her.

“Mara, I can’t find the paper on spatial-temporal models. I wanted to verify the equations you wrote on the board.”

She took a step toward the center of the room. His shoulders stayed still. “You saw the annotations. Are you questioning them?”

“No.”

“Then what are you verifying?”

“A process.”

He did not move closer. His body did not tell her not to move away.

She leaned against the counter. “You have a process for checking my work.”

“A general process. For anything I want to double-check.”

“How often does that happen?”

“I’m not sure.”

“Because you’re not checking often enough to notice.”

He smiled. “It might be time to look.”

The fridge was full of drinks, creamers, and a bowl of blackberries someone had taken from a local stand. Jonah reached for the blackberries.

“These are for human consumption,” Mara said. “They don’t check equations.”

“They’re good with water kefir.”

“If you want water kefir, ask Adrian.”

He picked up the bowl. “You’re in a sour mood today.”

“I have a theory about why.”

Jonah examined the fruit. “I’m not the reason.”

“Your work isn’t the reason.”

The bowl tilted as he set it back in the fridge and closed the door.

She pressed her back against the counter and crossed her arms. “I want to know what you’re verifying, Jonah. And I don’t want the answer to begin with ‘A process.’”

He stood on the other side of the door. His hands had not left the handles.

“Your work isn’t the problem.”

She waited.

“It’s not me either.” He closed his eyes briefly. “I’m verifying the process of trust. Is that what you wanted to hear?”

“No.”

“That’s an improvement.”

“Not much of one.”

He looked down at his hands, which had not left the fridge door. “I’m not the problem.”

She took another step back. “Is that a guess, or have you checked it?”

The door remained closed. “Have I earned the right to ask about the assumption that I am?”

“You’re not the assumption. You’re the man who did nothing to question it.”

“I don’t know how.”

He turned and walked into the main room. Mara listened to the chair scrape as he sat, then turned back to the coffee grounds. She set the compost outside and waited on the kitchen side of the door until she heard a vehicle start up and drive away.

Livia was at the main table when Mara came out. She had logged out of her phone. The light had moved farther into the room, leaving the back corner dark. Mara could see the rise and fall of Livia’s chest where the sweater hugged her.

“We’re late.” Livia closed her tablet. “We need to get back to work.”

Mara moved into the circle. Her chair scraped.

“Do you want to talk about Jonah?” Livia asked.

The chair remained where it was. “I want to talk about work.”

Livia nodded. “Then work.”

Mara crossed her arms. “You asked me to look for a process in his work. The process is him.”

“You think he’s the process.”

“It’s what I’m seeing. Can you give me a reason not to?”

Livia considered that. “Tell me again why you don’t trust him.”

Mara crossed and uncrossed her legs. “I don’t know how to make that question useful.”

“I’m not asking for your assumptions. I’m asking for the actual work you’ve done.”

“There’s the problem.”

“That you can’t separate the two?”

“That you think the assumption is the problem.”

“It might not be. But you can’t keep expecting me to see past the process to the man.” Livia’s voice remained even. Mara had not realized how much that cost. “If that’s the only option, then you might be wrong.”

“He’s the option. He’s the reason.”

“Not if you don’t have evidence.”

“I have evidence.” Mara raised her hands toward Livia. “Here’s the evidence: when the team left, he was standing inside my door while I was dumping the coffee grounds. He waited until I had finished and the door was still open.”

“So he had access to the room.”

“So he didn’t.”

“And?”

“And he did nothing.”

“He was being polite.”

“He was being complicit. You want me to trust him. I want to trust him. He won’t help.”

Livia’s eyes remained on Mara. “He wasn’t the one who helped.”

The kitchen door stayed in her face for half an hour before she realized what she had seen. Jonah had not left. The door was not behind her. She did not know when he had closed it or why. She had not expected a miracle. She did not think she had asked for one.

Livia did not mention that she had heard the door close.
