Livia sat at a side desk beside the data wall. The blue-red spectrum map expanded and contracted in response to her commands. Miriam worked on a tablet near the door and said nothing. The two men sitting beside her in the back row had begun to resemble sleep.

The session’s target group was in the main room, behind a two-way mirror. Mara could see them but not hear them. A narrow console ran along the observation room’s mirrored side. On the console’s main screen, the data stream collected itself in real time. Beneath it, the raw sensor data flowed in columns. A third window showed the alignment interface, where Jonah worked as the facilitator.

At the far end of the console, Mara monitored the data stream and the primary trace for artifacts. The session’s target group had arrived three weeks after she did. They were researchers, academics, and engineers who had signed up for a week’s immersion in alignment. They did not know they were the subjects of a longer study.

She had known when she signed the agreement, and again when she signed the contract. The place had paid more than enough to keep her from worrying about it.

The session had started at four. It was now seven-twelve. The stream’s primary trace was stable. The secondary trace—the one Livia was building from sensor data—remained noisy. Mara felt that noise in her face and hands and had not eaten anything solid since breakfast.

Livia rose. Her shoulders still carried the first night’s green dress, the only one she had worn at the pavilion since Mara arrived. She walked toward the mirror with slow, self-conscious steps. The green made her look like an imitation of a person, an observation that belonged in the stream. Mara did not speak it aloud.

The blue-red map rippled as Livia touched the mirror with both palms.

The target group rose, and the session ended. Mara heard doors close in the main room, and then a man’s voice in the hallway beyond. The voice was young and high. It sounded anxious.

“They’re leaving,” Mara said. “Livia should have gone with them.”

Livia looked back at her. “Why?”

“Because they’re leaving.”

“You’re annoyed I’m still here.”

“I am.”

She moved close enough for the scent of tea and resin to reach Mara. “What will you do when it’s just us?”

“Work.”

“You’ll watch the data flow, and you’ll notice which bits you want to keep. You’ll hear yourself describe it and know whether it sounds convincing. I’ll have to watch you, too, because I need a point of comparison.”

Mara turned back to the data stream. The room grew warmer.

“I can’t stop thinking about you.”

“Then think about someone else. There’s an entire cohort in the pavilion. Select one.”

Livia shifted closer. “That isn’t how it works.”

“Maybe you’re not as good at calibration as you think you are.”

“You’re scared.”

“No. I’m hungry. And I don’t want to be late for my own dinner.”

Livia pressed against her left side, and the mirror rippled with the contact. Mara did not move. The wanting was there: work, place, Livia’s hands, Livia’s mouth. She had not forgotten why. The answer remained the same.

“You don’t want to stop.” Livia’s voice was quiet, close.

Mara let her head turn. The wanting felt less like an obligation now that Livia had named it.

“I can’t afford to stop.”

Livia tilted her head to consider her.

“You can.”

The green dress disappeared around Mara. Livia’s hair brushed Mara’s neck; Mara felt the pressure of her body against her side. The mirror caught all of it, but only Mara saw.

Mara closed her eyes. “Can you afford this?”

“I can afford anything.”

“Not the real thing.”

Livia’s hands touched her neck and moved to her hair. Her hands were warm. Mara could not recall Livia being this warm before.

“I can afford this,” Livia said. “The real thing is a cost you choose.”

The work was there, but not the place. Mara could feel her own pulse between her fingers. Her breath shortened. The wanting returned in full, the same as the first night and every day after. She opened her eyes.

The green dress was gone. The chair opposite hers sat empty. Livia was gone.

Mara turned and saw her by the door.

“Sorry,” she said. “You can’t afford it.”

The observation room’s back wall had a door and a narrow window. Mara watched Livia walk toward the window, open it, and climb through.

“Livia,” she said.

Livia turned and smiled. “Yes, Mara.”

Mara felt her own mouth drop, and the wanting narrowed to one thing. “I need more coffee.”

Livia smiled again and dropped the other side of the window closed. The lock clicked.

Mara pressed her hands against the glass.

The room grew colder. She sat back down and continued processing the data stream. Miriam and the two men in the back row had already left. The monitors continued to run. The console remained open, but Jonah had closed the alignment interface. The stream and the primary trace still waited to be sealed.

Her left hand touched the place between her fingers where she had felt her pulse.

She ran the stream until the remaining artifacts were too faint to trace. Jonah entered at some point, watched her for ten minutes, and left without speaking.

By the time she was ready to seal the stream, the data wall was cold and dark. No one else remained. She closed the trace, exported the stream to the main repository, and pulled a sweater across her shoulders. Her cell phone had been charging at the side table. She switched it on, found three missed calls, and called back the most recent one.

“Hello.”

“Dad, it’s Mara.”

“Mara. How are you?”

“Fine. I was just working. Where are you?”

“I’m at the house. We’re having dinner in half an hour. Where are you?”

“I’m at work.”

“Then you should come.”

“You’re cooking for the two of you and then for me?”

“Your mother would have cooked. I’m reheating a pot pie from a place near here that she’s always talking about. Come eat with us. I’d rather be with you tonight than alone.”

“I’d rather be with you both.”

“Then come. The two of us can’t wait any longer.”

“You just said we’d have to reheat it.”

Her father laughed. “We could eat it cold.”

“You’ve never eaten anything cold.”

“I’d do it for you. You’re my daughter.”

Mara smiled. She did not mention the half-eaten bun sitting untouched in the conference room, and he did not ask. He would have taken the answer if he could have trusted it.

“I’m on my way.”

“I love you, Mara.”

The words made her swallow. She said them back and closed the cell phone. The empty observation room felt larger.

The exit waited where Livia had gone. Mara pulled on her jacket and went out into the dark.

The road to her parents’ house was downhill, but she took the back way so she could walk up first. The path began at the cedar deck behind the pavilion. It rose through a redwood forest into a grove of maples, then descended past the back gate of her parents’ backyard.
