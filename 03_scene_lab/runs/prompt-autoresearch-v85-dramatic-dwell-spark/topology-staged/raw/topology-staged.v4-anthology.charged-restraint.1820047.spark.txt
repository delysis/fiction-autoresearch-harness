 Mara followed, trying not to step on her hem.
She didn’t know where to look. In the lobby of a hotel she could have turned and faced the exit, ignoring Jonah, or stayed in the elevator while he left it, or found another route back to her room without walking along the wide, well-lighted halls, or pretended she was deaf and couldn’t hear the ring on her door. But this wasn’t an impersonal building. There was a road down the front drive to the main road; if they followed it for twenty minutes they’d find their motel, with a bright sign and a big parking lot and a clerk behind bulletproof glass, but that wouldn’t happen until they walked here, on this dark path under the trees, beside each other. She could step into the woods instead. Or pretend to be blind, and ask him to lead her. It was dark enough that no one would notice. He wouldn’t be able to see the tears if she cried.
She let her gaze follow his, moving over the tall grasses and ferns to the low branches of the fir trees, then up to the moonless sky. If they walked along the drive instead, their eyes could meet, and they could talk, but she didn’t want that. If they walked this path they’d be hidden among the trees. If they stayed at least three feet apart, he wouldn’t hear her breath, or her pulse, or the tears when they started. If he didn’t notice she was crying, it would be like not crying. She could pretend to be alone in the dark woods while he walked beside her.
Jonah kept walking. His left side was three feet to the left of hers, and she matched pace with him, the tips of their shoes about a foot apart on the ground, and didn’t look at him. He might be listening, though, to her breathing. He could tell when she wasn’t breathing normally.
A branch brushed her forearm as she walked past. She jumped, jerked sideways and stepped on her left heel, a hard crunch of gravel that could have been heard over the water rushing in the stream. She was glad to have something else to pay attention to; the ache in her foot was useful. Jonah’s footsteps stopped; there was a rustling sound beside her, and the sound of twigs breaking under his feet as he stepped sideways off the path. He wasn’t talking, but she heard him catch his breath. His lungs sounded fine, and his pulse too. He must have been watching the ferns under the fir trees, because he didn’t notice the tears.
Mara focused on the ground in front of her shoes, and walked as far away from him as the narrow path allowed. Her feet were stiff and hurt more after she’d stopped walking and started again.
