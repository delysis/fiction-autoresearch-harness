
“I was expecting someone else,” Mara said, still holding her ground, “someone more like a teacher.”
“A teacher of what?” the blond man asked.
“Of reading faces or something like that. She’s called Livia Sloane.”
“Yes.”
“I don’t think you are Livia.”
He smiled at her, slightly, like a cat with an open box. She had seen that expression in online videos.
She had studied dozens of those videos over the last week. None of them had actually prepared her for this.
“Well,” she said, “I’m glad that it’s not her. I don’t know about you, but if I’m going to be stuck in here all night, I’d rather look at you.”
The blond man let the smile play a little longer, then glanced away.
“I’m glad that I’m not the object of your attention.”
She realized suddenly how she was standing there in the doorway, so unselfconsciously watchful; how she had been watching him all along with this cold, frank stare, as though he were a display animal in a zoo or a criminal on the witness stand.
He was probably used to being stared at that way; it came with the territory. She felt a sudden tenderness and anger toward him for this, an urge to do something kind for him, and also an urge to hurt him.
