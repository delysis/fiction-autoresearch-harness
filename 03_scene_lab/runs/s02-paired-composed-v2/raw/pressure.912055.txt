Mara leaned her face against the side console and thought, *The clock is a suggestion.*

It was a bad prayer. She liked her prayers to rhyme, and the second line lacked an anchor. It was a bad prayer to a God who had never needed one.

Miriam adjusted the mic gain on the console while the group continued in the circle. Mara heard their words without seeing their faces: Livia’s precise consonants, Jonah’s low laugh, someone else’s exhale as they made a claim. They had taken off their shoes again, and the room sounded as close as the group would let her be without full membership.

They would let her be closer later, when Adrian said, “Mara?” and invited her to speak.

The session had begun with a paper from the Journal of Consciousness Studies. Someone—perhaps Livia—had highlighted two words on every page: MEASUREMENT PROBLEM. Jonah had proposed a quantum experiment to prove it was real, then claimed he was joking before anyone could applaud. Silas had called for a better name, and a dozen others had called him out for proposing an acronym.

Mara’s copy of the paper was annotated with Miriam’s comments. There was no space around the highlighted MEASUREMENT PROBLEM; a footnote had appeared in the margin; three sentences had been underlined twice.

Miriam looked over at her. The OBSERVER badge was gone. In its place, the COHORT badge said: MIRIAM HOLT, MEMBER.

“They’re running on borrowed time,” Miriam said.

Mara did not have time for sleep, which she knew was not how it worked. She had attended her first alignment session that morning—a reading group where the text’s claims were secondary to the room’s response—then spent the afternoon observing the calibration game for other fellows. The calibration was the hardest part, because every other session left her with something useful: a set of metrics from Adrian’s governance class, a model from Jonah’s design studio, Livia’s list of possible selves that moved through Mara’s mind like an echo. The calibration left her depleted and unable to explain why.

She told Miriam, “The density is higher.”

“You mean the intensity.”

“That would sound like an opinion.”

“You’ll do well here.”

Mara watched Silas fold his hands between his knees. *Jesus, help me want less.*

“Your hands are shaking.”

Mara turned her face toward the console. “The data density is higher.”

Miriam considered her. The pause did not feel like a judgment or an invitation. “I might have something you’d want to see.”

That was not how it worked. Mara waited.

Miriam opened the calibration run from four nights before. The screen displayed pulse and pressure for both Jonah and Mara. Two parallel traces at the start. Two traces separated by one degree before the hood went up.

“My own private deviation,” Miriam said. “The pressure interrupt you’ll notice. This was one of the reasons I asked to become a cohort observer. The data stream remains sealed to fellows for a month, but if I’d had access that night, I would have reported the interruption. It’s one of the reasons I applied here. If you wanted to know what she saw, you could ask me.”

Her face held no judgment, no calculation, no trace of wanting anything other than a clean investigation.

That did not make the offer easier. Mara remembered the moment she had changed the channel and wondered whether Miriam could see it in the trace.
