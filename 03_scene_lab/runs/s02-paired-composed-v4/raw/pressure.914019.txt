The table shook with a distant footfall.

A man named Felix came into the lab, slowed by the door closing in his face. “I don’t have a badge. They must have forgotten to issue one.” He sat beside Mara with an ease she did not possess.

Mara recognized him as the fellow who had not moved in the fire circle. His attention had stayed on her during the calibration game, more like an audience member than a participant. He had not appeared since that first night.

Livia put a hand on Jonah’s elbow. “We’ll close the session in five minutes. Please don’t go.”

Jonah’s face remained neutral. His hands stopped moving.

“Five minutes.”

Livia looked at Mara. “Can he be persuaded?”

“You’re trying to persuade me to try to persuade him to try to persuade you.”

Livia made the sound of someone eating a biscuit.

Jonah saved the file and closed the application.

Livia exhaled, turned the screen toward Felix, and began explaining the session. It was the same talk she gave the full room before each alignment task: the value of recognizing meaning outside speech; the importance of making claims testable before turning them into rules; the necessity of admitting error.

“We begin with a test,” Livia said. “A prompt, a touch, a signal. The sender holds the sensation without description. The receiver makes a claim. The sender gives feedback. The observer checks the stream against the claims.”

“I think I need something closer to first principles.” Felix tapped a slender finger against the cedar tabletop. “That sounds like divination.”

“The method is transparent,” Livia said.

“And the results?”

“Reproducible.”

“Demonstrably?”

“Or we fail.” Livia looked at Mara. “Our current hypothesis concerns a blind spot. Can we teach a receiver to predict where someone will place their hand without that person knowing they are being observed?”

“That’s the question?”

“That’s the control.”

“Is it a useful skill?”

Livia’s face held interest before the question reached it. Mara realized she was watching Livia answer the stranger rather than merely explaining the task to him.

“It’s a test,” Livia said. “We run tests. You know that.”

“Of course I know that.”

Mara could not tell how Felix’s voice held both laughter and discomfort. His fingers tapped on the table again.

“What if somebody makes an accurate prediction but it isn’t reproduced?” Livia asked. “Do we call it a coincidence? Do we try to control for a variable we don’t know exists?”

“If I understand your question, I would call it an outlier.”

Livia’s eyes widened. It might have been laughter. It might have been pleasure, but not pleasure in Felix’s answer.

Mara’s stomach churned with dinner that did not exist. She could have blamed the churn on the experiment. Instead she blamed it on the man across from Livia.

“Yes,” Livia said, “outlier is the word I’m thinking of.”

“Outliers are still data,” Mara said.

Livia turned toward her with a quick smile. The smile included Mara’s answer, but it did not come from it.

“And if we can find the variable that produced it,” Felix said, “we have a more accurate model.”

The smile vanished. Livia’s attention narrowed until she resembled her photographs again.
