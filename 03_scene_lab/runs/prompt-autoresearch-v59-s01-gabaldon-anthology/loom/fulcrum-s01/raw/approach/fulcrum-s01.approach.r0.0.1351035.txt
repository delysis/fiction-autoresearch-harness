“I know,” she said with a smile. “That’s what I mean—it’s not like a regular job for you, is it?”
“I’ve got a job.” He was opening the trunk of his car with a remote device; she caught him looking at her with interest and thought, he means the interview.
“What do you do?” She pulled her suitcase from the trunk.
“I work for the F.B.I.,” he said, simply. “In the Behavioral Analysis Unit.”
She was a psychologist, herself, and had known about the Unit. It was an elite group of investigators, known colloquially as mind-hunters. They specialized in analyzing the behavior of violent criminals, using psychological profiling to identify characteristics common to offenders of a particular type. Most of them were former or current cops. She couldn’t imagine why one would come to Fulcrum.
“Do you … bring work here with you?” she asked, thinking, I can’t picture that.
“Sometimes,” he said, smiling. “I’ve been on the job a long time. They don’t call me very often, these days.”
He must be pretty good at what he did to have made it that far up in the organization. He’d certainly not been lying about having done the work for long.
“Did you bring any files?” she asked.
He shrugged. “No files,” he said. “But I brought the case.”
They stepped out from the shade of the car, and Mara heard the sharp intake of breath behind her as Hal caught sight of the buildings. The sun was falling, slanting gold and red across the grass on the terrace and through the tall glass walls. The white structure at the top gleamed, and she saw, not the light, but the reflection of the water that had fallen down its length and pooled in the shallow pool at the bottom of its vertical drop.
“That’s beautiful,” Hal said quietly. He gestured at the trees to one side of the lawn. “But it’s the trees that got me.”
“What about them?” She was already halfway across the lawn, looking at the trees on the other side. The sun had already gone behind them, but the light played upon their leaves, turning them to gold and a deep purple.
“The way they change color,” he said simply. “It’s magical.”
“It is,” she said, and smiled at him. She meant it; the whole setting was magical.
The glass of the front doors made a faint chink as they pushed open into the dim space beyond, and an old man with a mop rose from a chair and bowed to her, murmuring a quiet hello.
“Hello,” she said back. “Nice to see you.” She went past him; the lobby was large and bare, except for a polished concrete fountain at one end and a table beside a set of stairs to a mezzanine. There was no one at the desk; she stopped in the door to the room beyond, looking around.
