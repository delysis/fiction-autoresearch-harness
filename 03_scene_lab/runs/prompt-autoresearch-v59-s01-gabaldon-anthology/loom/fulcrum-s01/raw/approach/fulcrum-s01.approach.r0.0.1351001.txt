 She was blushing like an idiot, she knew; what business was it of hers, anyway? It came to her in that moment how strange this would all have seemed even two days earlier. If you had told her then she was going to be here now, she wouldn’t have believed it. She said as much to him. He nodded, with that thoughtful smile.
“I know.”
He didn’t seem to think it necessary to explain further. But the air was becoming suddenly, unpleasantly warm inside the car, and she opened her window without thinking. The late-summer evening was warm, though not humid, and there was something of sea tang in the wind.
She got out of the car before it came to a full stop, leaving him to carry her bags and find a parking place. He smiled as he saw her go, she thought. It had nothing to do with her looks. It wasn’t her good looks that had got her this job; it wasn’t those that made his eyes stay on her face.
She walked slowly, not wanting to rush at it—but not wanting to stand and stare, either. The glass walls were completely reflective, and showed a small, narrow woman in black jeans and an open-necked yellow shirt, her dark hair shining in the light of the setting sun, which was now just peeping over the ridge on the far side. She couldn’t see inside.
There was no one else about. She paused to light a cigarette, then looked around again, taking stock. There was something unusual about this place. The low glass building stood in stark contrast to the forest it rose from. There was no sign of life; it could have been a deserted office building on some strange planet.
The buildings were clustered together, all of them seeming to be made of concrete and glass. There were four other buildings behind the one she was in, each one set back a few feet from its neighbors. She was looking down at the parking lot, with one long row of cars parked nose to tail, facing away from her. A paved road ran parallel to the parking lot and curved around to the left, heading up toward the ridge. There was a large redwood grove to the right of the building; the sun shone through the trees, casting shadows on the grass and making the trees seem like giants standing guard over the parking lot.
She could see no one else at all. There were no other cars in sight, no sign of life in or around any of the buildings. She wondered what it would be like to be inside, looking out at this strange little world. She found herself imagining what it must be like to walk through the forest on the far side of the parking lot, where she could hear the sound of rushing water.
She didn’t want to imagine that.
She hesitated for a moment at the glass doors, feeling the pull of two opposite impulses: to get inside and find out what was going on here—or to walk away while she still had time.
She went in.
The lobby was cool, with an atmosphere of quiet efficiency; it reminded her of nothing so much as a university office complex. A long white desk ran along the far wall, behind which was a room that could have been used either as a waiting room or as a small seminar area. The door was open; there was no one in sight. She saw a bank of telephones on the desk and picked up the nearest one. It was dead.
“You must be Ms. Stewart,” a woman’s voice said from behind her.
