 She’d been wondering about that; she wanted to feel sure—certainty was one of the things she felt she had in very short supply these days. But so much of what she thought about Fulcrum seemed to depend on what she thought of other people there; how much could she really make up her mind about it?
“Well,” he said, giving her an intent look. “I would say this: that it’s an opportunity. That’s what I told my son—I told him to think of it as an opportunity, and don’t squander it. Don’t go wasting time around the pool.”
Hal had a son here? She didn’t know why she was so surprised. Hal didn’t look old enough to have a grown son; he didn’t look rich enough either—that was her own prejudice, of course, she knew it, but she found herself wondering what kind of scholarship he’d gotten that would allow his son to spend a year here.
“If he got any work done at all, though,” Hal went on, giving the car a light nudge as the automated gate swung open to let them in. “That’s what matters. If he got anything at all out of the year here, that was the money well spent.”
So he did have to pay for his son. The boy might have gotten some sort of scholarship—Fulcrum offered them—but it probably wasn’t enough to pay the full price. Hal had said “work”—was this a place where they could come and work at things they’d never even known they needed to work on? Was that why they came?
“But there’s a lot of young people out here,” he said, squinting down the drive at the lights glowing ahead. “Lot of very bright young people, but no life experience. You see, it’s like this…” He waved one hand vaguely. “…they have great brains, and they think with them all the time. But if you have no experience in life, then there are things you don’t understand—things you can only understand by living.”
His hands moved in a complex pattern of explanation, which she might have understood, but she wasn’t really paying much attention to him at the moment—she was paying attention to what he was saying.
It was the way he talked that made it plain as day. He had lived with this; he had thought about it; he knew it inside and out. He might be able to explain it—might, given time, tell her just exactly what he meant, but he couldn’t possibly make her understand it—not if she didn’t already know it.
She didn’t know what it was. But she knew what he meant.
