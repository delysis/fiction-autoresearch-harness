Mara’s fingers tightened on a glass of lemon-ginger tea. The warmth seeped through her fingertips, but it was not the kind that removed chill.

Adrian Voss had closed the screen between them and gone to speak to two fellows about their morning scores. His hair had acquired a loose wave from too much time indoors. Livia Sloane sat opposite in a green dress that changed her shoulders into something Mara could not name. Both of them watched Mara without appearing to.

A dozen other fellows moved through the open pavilion toward the kitchen, the exercise yards, or their apartments. Elias, who had let Livia read him a week earlier, brushed past Mara without looking at her. Her hands itched for a keyboard she did not have.

She took another sip of tea.

Livia poured a cup for herself, amber against the pale green ceramic. “Are you all right?”

Mara took the cup. “Data density. This many simultaneous streams feels like trying to read three people at once.”

Livia’s mouth twisted. “Your data density problem has no correlation with the thing you said when Adrian left.”

Mara felt her face warm. The tea helped. She managed an answer without spilling it.

“I wasn’t ready to be read that well.”

“You’re afraid I’ll see that you’re good but not exceptional.”

“No.”

“And that I’ll explain the fear as ambition.”

Mara touched the lid of her suitcase beside the couch. “You already did.”

“The first time, the wanting was abstract. Now the place is real. We’re real. The admission matters differently.” Livia’s attention remained on her face, waiting for Mara to explain it or pretend she had not felt it.

Mara did not. She stared into the tea, which was green, and waited for her breathing to level.

Livia took a sip. “The fellowship is a status game. You can’t afford to be that fragile.”

Mara looked up. Livia was watching her through one raised eyebrow, her hands folded neatly around the cup.

Mara set her own down. “So I’m here to get fixed.”

Livia gave a small laugh. “No. You’re here to get exceptional. Or to prove that you already are.”

She moved to stand. The door opened before Mara could say anything more.

Jonah carried a silver terminal that had been removed from its rack in the middle of the exercise yard. He looked as if he had carried something heavier than the machine’s three pounds. He glanced once at Livia, then at the tea between Mara’s hands, and set the terminal on the couch beside Mara.

The display came on in a shimmer of live traces. Mara knew the one closest to her. That was where her pressure, pulse, breathing, and room audio entered the machine before the telemetry sealed itself for later review.

“I came to learn the password.” He leaned toward the screen. “And to check that we weren’t too generous about data integrity.”

Livia’s smile sharpened. “You’re worried we might actually have useful secrets.”

“I’m worried you can’t keep the useless ones.”
