

The palm of his hand felt warm against Mara’s fingers, dry, smooth-skinned except for calluses where his tools had carved their own imprint. He didn’t let her pull away or rush ahead or hurry him along. When she stopped to wipe sweat from her neck, he kept their hands joined and just waited beside her.

When she was ready, he followed at the same easy pace.

She felt the connection through his hand for nearly a full quarter-mile, a steady weight that carried her forward without effort. She felt him step forward to close gaps and slow down if she stopped to watch birdsong flash in the trees. He held the balance so gently that she noticed only when he eased out.

When he let go she stood still for a moment, palm on the wood rail of the steps leading down to her street. Her arm felt lighter, but it swung in his rhythm. Jonah had left her carrying his beat, his body’s forward momentum. She stepped out and followed it up to the front door.

The lock released with a quiet click. As she opened the door she looked up at him and smiled. The smile turned into a wave as she stepped back inside.

The door clicked closed behind her. When she turned it was closed, the steps empty. He had waited only until she made it safely inside.

Mara leaned against the door and let the cold metal frame cool her shoulders. It felt good to be in her own space. Good to be out of the lab. She hadn’t cried again since she called him “doctor” in the session and heard herself say it. The words had been almost a reflex. They were something she used to protect herself from getting too close, from wanting things she couldn’t have, from risking vulnerability and rejection.

She thought about their walk. Thought about the way she had let him hold her hand without flinching. She wasn’t sure why it felt so good to walk beside him when she usually kept a distance between them. Usually she was too afraid to let herself want things she couldn’t have. But tonight…tonight she felt safe and wanted.

Mara pulled back from the door frame and walked into the living room. The dim light from the kitchen filtered into the dark space where she collapsed on the worn couch. She stared at the ceiling and thought about the session, the experiment, how she’d acted like a patient instead of a fellow researcher, how Jonah had responded with kindness instead of frustration or pity.

He hadn’t diagnosed her or tried to help her. He’d just listened and let her work through things on her own. That was better than being fixed or cured. That was better than being saved. That was better than being patronized or pitied. That was better than being alone.

She sighed and turned onto her side, facing the empty room. The couch creaked under her weight but felt like home. She closed her eyes and let the darkness take her.
