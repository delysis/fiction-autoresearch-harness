The Institute for Attentive Behaviors had been named “Fulcrum” in order to conceal what it studied. Few understood that studying attentive behavior was the same as studying love. Love, as Fulcrum defined it, was both an intention and a set of behaviors. The intention could be for anything—a dog, a child, a stranger’s suffering—but love was not a word until you acted on the intention. Love’s behavior was listening.
By listening I mean a simple, embodied choice. We don’t think to listen; we just do it. Listening happens before words, as when a baby moves its head to find a sound or an adult turns toward a loved person’s touch. You can listen even when there’s nothing to hear. Just breathe, let your body settle, and focus on the felt space in front of you. You don’t need to think or speak. You don’t need to look or touch. You just listen.
One of Fulcrum’s basic skills was listening to each other’s hands. This practice of touch-listening taught me how a simple choice could change everything.

It was the night before New Year’s Eve. I had been at Fulcrum for three weeks. My first group lab session was not going well. I did not know that until it ended.
