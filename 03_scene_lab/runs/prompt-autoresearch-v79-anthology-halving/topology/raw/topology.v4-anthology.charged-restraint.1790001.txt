 The air was cool. It pressed into her hot lungs and didn’t seem to help. She needed to be cold: cold enough not to feel anything.
His pace was neither fast nor slow. His eyes stayed on the ground as he walked. His feet made little thudding noises on the pavement, rhythmic without trying. Mara didn’t want to walk with him. She wanted to fall down at the base of the steps and cry and then keep sleeping until morning. She needed him to walk with her, or she would stop.
The silence between them was a tangible wall. They reached the end of the driveway. He didn’t say anything at all as he turned right instead of left toward the main highway. No destination seemed in mind—just the opposite direction of where they should have been going.
Mara matched his footsteps automatically, and then they had crossed the street and were walking on the sidewalk under the trees along the road. She took a breath to say something, to offer a comment or question, but he turned his head away and let the silence grow louder. It pressed against her ears, a barrier between them.
His slow breathing sounded steady and sure, as though he could go on like this indefinitely, but he kept it to himself; he didn’t offer her anything but silence and that regular breath.
Mara wondered if he was still in shock. She knew how he must feel: the disorientation after a night of sleep deprivation, the lack of time to sort through emotions. No one would talk about what had happened because it had just happened.
He kept his eyes on the ground and didn’t look up once as they passed other cars on the road. He seemed to be breathing at random, with no sense of sequence or purpose, yet he walked forward, always turning right when he reached a corner. He had a destination; it was not the hotel where she was staying tonight, but neither was it in sight nor did he acknowledge its presence in the world.
The silence was a weight on Mara’s shoulders, heavier than any other she could think of, and Jonah seemed to enjoy it: he was taking his time, breathing easily, as though there were no one else around and nothing to distract him from his own thoughts.
The air grew cooler as they continued on through the empty streets, and Mara was shivering now, unable to stop herself. He had always been a slow talker; he had always been more interested in what she thought than what she felt.
Now he looked at her with something like pity on his face—she knew the emotion but couldn’t remember seeing it on him before—and she wanted to ask him why he was being silent, why he was going so slowly. Why he had said nothing about what had happened tonight.
He wouldn’t answer; he never did. And suddenly Mara remembered that they had gone out for drinks once, and he had been polite and quiet, and she had not tried to say much herself because she thought it made him uncomfortable. But tonight…tonight his silence felt deliberate, as though it were part of some larger plan that he had not shared with her.
The streets were empty; there was nothing for them to see except their own reflections in the windows along either side of them. And then suddenly Jonah said, “You can walk alone, can’t you?” She opened her mouth and shut it again; she felt like a child caught doing something wrong by a parent whose anger was worse than anything else.
“I don’t—” She stopped. His gaze was fixed on the street ahead, and his back was straight, and his steps were regular and measured.
