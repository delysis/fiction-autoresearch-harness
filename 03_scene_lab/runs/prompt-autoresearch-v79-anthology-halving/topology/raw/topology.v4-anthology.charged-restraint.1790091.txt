 He was moving away from her. If she wanted to keep up, she would have to catch up. She took a deeper breath and did it.
The pavement was cold under her shoes. The air felt cooler than the stones; she could see her breath. It was only a few degrees below freezing.
She had forgotten the wind, had barely noticed it before. She shivered as it caught the front of her coat. It felt like fingers.
They walked in silence down the wide, curving driveway. Fulcrum’s lights were behind them: soft white squares fading as she glanced backward.
The cold air felt good. A slight breeze made the oak branches shift. Leaves rattled and squeaked against one another; she heard small thumps as they dropped to the ground. She saw one of her own steps before she took it. It was dark around her, and she was glad that she couldn’t see Jonah’s face.
Her left ankle protested. She shifted her weight onto her right foot, then back, testing.
She had never hurt an ankle before. The pain wasn’t severe. The ankle didn’t look swollen. If it ached in the morning, she would walk slowly. Or find one of those rented scooters like the one she had seen in San Francisco last year.
Or take a day off, which could mean sleeping until noon. It could mean working in bed on her own research notes. She imagined opening an expensive bottle of wine with dinner, though she wasn’t sure she had anything that would go with pizza.
“We’ll have to walk up Main,” Jonah said. His voice made her start; she nearly bumped into him. He was walking slowly enough to keep pace with her, which meant that he must know how she felt. He hadn’t looked back at her once since they left Fulcrum.
Her head and shoulders ached. Her entire body was too hot; the cold outside seemed good. It made her feel less exposed, as though there were armor between them now.
She had only a short way to walk. She didn’t say this aloud, but she thought it. She wondered what Jonah thought of her silence, of her failure to initiate conversation, even when it was necessary to say that he was right. The route he had chosen would not take her home, but she didn’t say this either.
She couldn’t think of anything to say. She hadn’t planned tonight’s session; she was barely speaking now because she couldn’t get a new word order out. She wondered whether she sounded as flat and dull as she felt. She wondered what Jonah wanted her to say. If she were able to read him like an experimenter, she might have some idea. If he had been willing to play a game.
She wanted to ask him why he walked with her, but she couldn’t force the question out. He had gone along with tonight’s session because it would be bad science if he hadn’t, but if he were going through the motions again just because she was walking with him now, she wanted him to say so. If he knew what she wanted to know, he was free to tell her.
She wished she could ask him about himself: why he had come here tonight. She might have expected to find that he was lonely or even desperate, but he wasn’t like that. She wanted to ask him that but didn’t have enough words, not after tonight. She didn’t know what else she could do.
