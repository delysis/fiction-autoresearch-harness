Mara felt like a broken bird, blown off its perch by a howling gust of wind, shivering with the force of it but unable to get under shelter, just barely holding on to the will to live by flapping at the icy air in the hope that it might hold her up. She had been shaking all over, her hands clutched tightly around a Styrofoam cup of lukewarm coffee, trying to still the trembling of her fingers and her teeth. The wind howled around the building, and the rain poured from the sky. Her boots were soaked and her skirt stuck to her calves in damp, clammy folds.
She had no choice but to get up. She was a researcher at Fulcrum, a private psychotechnology institute in a remote corner of northern California. Fulcrum’s purpose was to study the nature of human attention, in particular the process by which people become aware of each other’s nonverbal signals—things like facial expressions, eye movements, posture, gesture, the timing of actions. The scientists and research associates who worked at the institute were all trained in the conscious perception of those things, to such an extent that their unconscious awareness of the cues became hyperactive.
