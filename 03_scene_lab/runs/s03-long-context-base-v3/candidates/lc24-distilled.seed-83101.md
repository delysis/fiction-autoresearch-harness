Mara stood in the dark aisle, one hand resting against the cedar plank that divided the chapel from the research wing. Her other hand held a glass of water. On her forehead, the memory of a kiss.
A clock ticked from an unseen console. It had taken three people and two hours to disable the chime, but the ticking had survived the operation.
“I can remove the clock face,” Adrian said behind her. “That will stop the ticking.”
Mara turned the glass. Water had condensed inside.
“Please don’t.”
“You’re not the only one who hears it.”
She pressed her lips against the glass and tasted a cold breath of distilled California.
“What is this place supposed to feel like?” she asked.
“Unsettling,” Adrian said.
Mara looked up.
The glass chapel had been designed by somebody who believed in angles. Light from the chapel wall washed the ceiling, and light from the ceiling washed the wall; where the two beams met, the walls appeared to vanish. The chapel’s floor was a long cedar plank that ended at a glass table. Inside the table, four monitors displayed live traces from the Fulcrum alignment run: Mara’s pulse, breath, posture, and the filtered wrist-contact stream.
Jonah had not built this display. A different hand had arranged the monitors into a cross and mounted them in the glass table where they could be seen but not touched.
Mara took the last step toward the glass table.
The traces had not changed since the run began at 9:30. Her pulse stayed flat. The breath trace was a series of tiny peaks. The contact stream remained blank.
“When it says no pulse,” she asked, “is there no pulse?”
Adrian’s shadow lengthened against the glass. “If we want to stop a trace, we have to build the machine that lies when we tell it to.”
Mara looked down at the blank contact stream.
“That seems like a lot of effort for one trace.”
Adrian’s thumb moved once across the glass. “It’s a lot of effort for any trace.”
The ticking continued.
Mara put the glass on the table.
“If the clock is so loud,” she said, “why don’t you put the console in another room?”
“We need you to hear the time.”
She looked at him. “Does your God demand that much accuracy?”
Adrian’s shadow did not change shape. “No. I do.”
“How convenient for your God.”
He did not answer. Mara pressed her palm against the glass table, warming the place where a clock face had been.
“I told you I could remove it,” he said.
“I’m not objecting.”
Adrian’s shadow lengthened across the floor.
“The calibration game was a demonstration. The overnight diagnostic was an invitation. This is a test.”
“I didn’t ask for it.”
“You accepted it.”
“I accepted access. Not this.”
His thumb moved across the glass again. “You’ll decide what is real.”
The ticking continued. Mara saw it in the shadow of Adrian’s hand.
“You don’t get to choose,” she said.
“We chose you.”
She put her weight against the cedar partition. The cedar smelled of pine pitch and new wood, but it did not flex.
“Then maybe I’m not what you wanted,” she said.
“Then maybe we are.”
His shadow did not move. Mara kept her hand against the glass. The glass began to warm.
The ticking continued.
“You said you needed me to hear the time.”
“Yes.”
“Why?”
Adrian’s shadow did not change shape.
“I’m not telling you,” he said.
She looked at him. “You can’t.”
“Maybe.”
“But you’ll still demand I tell you.”
His thumb moved across the glass again.
“Yes.”
The ticking continued.
Mara pressed her palm against the glass. The glass began to warm.
“You don’t have to do this,” she said.
“I know.”
“Then why are you doing it?”
Adrian’s shadow did not move.
“Because I can,” he said.
The ticking continued.
